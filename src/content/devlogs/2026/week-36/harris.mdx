The runtime architecture arrived at its current shape over the last two weeks, and this week I wrote it down properly. Not as a sales artifact — as a contract the rest of the team can build against.

### The components

- **Planner** — turns a resolved project into an execution plan: a directed structure of steps with explicit inputs and expected outputs.
- **Scheduler** — decides what runs when, subject to declared capacity and ordering constraints.
- **Supervisor** — owns step lifecycle: start, monitor, terminate on failure, apply policy.
- **Collector** — gathers artifacts and outcomes into a run record.

### The principle of the split

Planner treats logic. Scheduler treats economics. Supervisor treats physics. Collector treats history. Mixing any two is how runtimes become impossible to reason about, so the boundaries are strict.

### Two decisions worth recording

First, plans are materialized before execution. We don't stream work in — the whole plan exists up front, which makes retries and partial failures tractable. Second, supervision is a first-class component rather than something scattered through execute code. Every step has an unambiguous owner at every moment.

The design is deliberately boring. The interesting part starts next week, when these components become code with the pipeline implementation.
